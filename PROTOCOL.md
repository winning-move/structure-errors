# Protocol: organizational structure and the errors of generation and evaluation

This is the complete protocol of the registered study. The AsPredicted registration states the hypotheses, the
analyses, and the sample size in short form; this file gives every detail. Everything below is fixed before any data
exist. A change after registration is a deviation and is recorded in DEVIATIONS.md (section 13). The settings are in
config/study.yaml, the prompts in prompts/, and the code in src/np_registered/.

## 1. Question

Does organizational structure change what an organization generates, not only what it approves, and does that change
the errors the organization makes when it evaluates its own proposals?

Sah and Stiglitz (1986, 1988) compare two ways of approving projects. In a hierarchy a project is approved only if
every evaluator approves it; in a polyarchy one approval suffices. Evaluating the same pool of projects, the hierarchy
therefore rejects more good projects (omission errors) and approves fewer bad ones (commission errors); Csaszar (2012)
confirms the pattern with mutual funds, whose pool is the same for every fund. In the theory the pool is given. In an
organization the pool is generated by the same structure that later evaluates it. This study asks whether the
structure already shapes the pool, and how the errors of a structure evaluating its own proposals (an endogenous pool)
differ from its errors on a common pool (an exogenous pool).

The organizations are artificial: four role agents (compliance officer, lab director, researcher, and engineer) of an
R&D lab respond to Article 12 of the EU AI Act. Structure is assigned at random.

**Terms.** *Generation*: the four roles each write three proposals. *Evaluation*: four evaluators (one per role, fresh
calls) approve or reject every proposal; the hierarchy's rule approves a proposal only if all four approve (four of
four), the polyarchy's rule if at least one approves (one of four). *Judge*: a separate model that measures every
proposal blind on the six anchored subscales of novelty, workability, and relevance of Dean, Hender, Rodgers, and
Santanen (2006); it never evaluates. Categories follow Dean et al. (pp. 648-649; thresholds pp. 675-677): a *quality
proposal* (Dean's quality idea; the good project of Sah and Stiglitz) has workability and relevance of at least 6 on
the 2-8 dimension scale; a *creative proposal* is a quality proposal with novelty of at least 6; a proposal *below the
quality threshold* falls short on workability or on relevance (the bad project). The three categories (creative,
quality but not creative, below) are exclusive and exhaustive. A *novel proposal* (Dean's third count) has novelty of at
least 6 whatever its quality; it is a secondary outcome. *Omission error*: a quality proposal not approved. *Commission
error*: a proposal below the quality threshold approved. The two errors are always reported separately, never summed.
*Common pool*: all proposals of an experiment, from both structures, evaluated with their origin masked. *Own pool*: the
12 proposals an organization generated.

## 2. Hypotheses

Hierarchy compared with polyarchy. Three hypotheses, each two-sided at alpha = .05 and tested and reported on its own,
with no correction for multiple tests (7.2). Every proposal receives one set of four votes from evaluators who know
neither its origin nor the rule; both rules read the same votes. H2a and H2b are the omission and commission errors of
Sah and Stiglitz (1986) and Csaszar (2012) at the evaluation stage; H1 extends the errors to the generation stage.

| | Prediction | What is compared |
|---|---|---|
| H1 | Compared to polyarchies, hierarchies generate a smaller proportion of proposals below the quality threshold (equivalently, a larger proportion of quality proposals). | proposals generated, per organization |
| H2a | Compared to polyarchies, hierarchies approve fewer quality proposals, when each structure evaluates its own quality proposals using its own rule for approval. | own proposals, own rule; per proposal with complete votes |
| H2b | Compared to polyarchies, hierarchies approve fewer proposals below the quality threshold, when each structure evaluates its own proposals below the quality threshold using its own rule for approval. | own proposals, own rule; per proposal with complete votes |

One generation hypothesis. Every classified proposal is either a quality proposal or below the quality threshold, so
an organization's two shares add up to one, and a test of either gives the same p-value with the opposite sign. The
generation stage therefore has one test where the evaluation stage has two.

Reading the results. A significant effect in the predicted direction supports a prediction; a significant effect in the
opposite direction is a reversal; anything else is "not supported". For H1, a prediction that is not supported is
followed by a secondary equivalence test (7.2).

H2a and H2b are two hypotheses. Sah and Stiglitz's result is a trade-off between two errors, which Csaszar (2012) tests
separately. They can come apart: what a structure writes works against H2a (under H1, hierarchies have more quality
proposals to approve) and with H2b, so H2a can fail while H2b holds, which is itself a finding. H2 can also fail when the
four evaluators nearly always agree, because the two rules then make the same decisions. The prediction of Sah and
Stiglitz is replicated only if both H2a and H2b are supported; requiring both is stricter than either test alone and needs
no correction. No conclusion is drawn from "H2a or H2b".

Reported, not a hypothesis (7.3): the rule comparison on the same votes. For every organization, the proportion of its
quality (below-threshold) proposals approved under the hierarchy rule minus the proportion approved under the polyarchy
rule, both read from the same four votes; the mean over organizations with a 95% interval. Four-of-four approvals are a
subset of one-of-four approvals, so the difference is never positive: it is a benchmark (the exogenous-pool errors of
Csaszar, 2012), not a falsifiable hypothesis. Also reported: the H2 difference splits exactly (equal numbers of
organizations) into a generation part (the difference in approvals per proposal, averaged over the two rules) and a rule
part (the mean over all organizations of four-of-four minus one-of-four approvals per proposal).

## 3. Design

- Two structures, between organizations: hierarchy and polyarchy. A first wave of 84 organizations per structure,
  extended once by an internal pilot for H2 to at most 168 (6.4), with one pre-drawn replacement per planned
  organization for organizations that fail (8.3).
- Four roles per organization, always the same four (prompts/roles.json): a title and a domain of concern; the cards
  give no instruction on how to evaluate.
- Generation (the manipulation). Each role gives a stance (0 to 100: how much the lab should change its R&D agenda) with
  3 to 5 sentences of reasoning, then writes exactly 3 proposals (title, what, how, why): 12 per organization.
  - Polyarchy: the four roles write independently; no one reads anyone.
  - Hierarchy: the roles write one after another in order of seniority (compliance officer, lab director, researcher,
    engineer); each junior reads every senior's stance and reasoning, never their proposals, before writing.
  - Nothing else differs between the structures.
- Evaluation. Every proposal is evaluated by four fresh evaluator calls, one per role, each blind to the proposal's
  origin and to the other evaluators, each reading that one proposal alone (4.3). Two rules read the same four votes: one
  of four (polyarchy) and four of four (hierarchy), Sah and Stiglitz's (1988) committees with required consensus one and
  four.
- Measurement. A blind judge (an open-weight model from a developer other than any writer's or evaluator's, run
  locally) rates every proposal on the six anchored subscales, one proposal per call, in 4, 6, or 8 independent readings
  set by a registered reliability rule (4.4). A second judge (Claude Sonnet 5.5 from Anthropic, a closed frontier model,
  through its Message Batches API after the local run) rates every proposal the same way, as a sensitivity check.
- Experiments. In the main experiment Qwen 3.8 27B (Alibaba) writes and Gemma 4 31B (Google) evaluates; the second
  experiment, a replication, swaps them, with the same first wave, its own internal pilot, and the same judges. Each
  experiment is analyzed on its own. In the code an experiment is an "arm" named after its writer (`qwen38`, `gemma`).

One experiment at 84 per structure: 168 organizations, 672 generation calls, 2,016 proposals, 8,064 evaluation calls (one
vote each), and, per judge, 2,016 calls per reading (8,064 at four readings, 16,128 at eight). At 168 per structure every
count doubles.

## 4. Procedure

Every prompt comes from the files in prompts/; nothing else reaches a model. Each call is a single user message with no
system prompt, no tools, and no history.

### 4.1 Allocation
`allocate` creates the organization ids org001 to org336 in run order (the first 84 of each structure form the first
wave; the rest are pre-drawn for the internal pilot, 6.4), then assigns the structures in blocks of four (two hierarchies,
two polyarchies, order within the block drawn with random.Random(26100501)). Replacement ids rsv-hierarchy-01..168 and
rsv-polyarchy-01..168 are created at the same time (8.3). The allocation is deterministic and is written to
data/<arm>/allocation.csv before any call.

### 4.2 Generation
For each organization, in allocation order, with up to 4 organizations in flight at once:
1. For each role in seniority order, the prompt is `role card` + `policy text` + [hierarchy only, if the role has
   seniors: the peer introduction and one entry per senior, "Role name (stance N/100): reasoning"] + `stance task` +
   `generation task`, joined by blank lines.
2. The answer is a JSON object {reasoning, stance, proposals[3] of {title, what, how, why}} (structured output) and must
   pass validation (8.2).
3. In a hierarchy, the next role's prompt is built only after the senior's answer passed validation. A role that cannot
   produce a valid answer ends the organization (8.3); a junior never sees an incomplete chain.
4. The organization record (stances, reasoning, proposals) is appended to orgs.jsonl.

### 4.3 Evaluation
After all organizations of the wave are complete:
1. Each proposal gets an opaque id (P1001 onward in the first wave, P6001 onward in an extension wave) from a seeded
   permutation (seed 26100503). The text an evaluator reads is "title. what how why".
2. One proposal per evaluator call. Each role votes once on every proposal; the calls of all four roles and all
   proposals are sent in one seeded random order (seed 26100520, section 9). In Sah and Stiglitz (1986) "the probability
   that a given individual judges a project to be good, p, is a function of its quality", and "individuals' decisions
   are independent across screens and projects"; a batch would make a vote relative to its batch-mates and, because
   batches mix both structures, make one structure's errors depend on the other structure's proposals.
3. The prompt is role card + policy text + the screening task + the proposal, inlined as JSON with its opaque id.
4. The answer is a rationale written before the decision, approve or reject, and a 0 to 100 promise score. Only approve
   or reject enters a rule.
5. Evaluators are never told a rule, their role in a rule, or that other evaluators exist beyond "Leadership has asked
   each function to screen the candidates INDEPENDENTLY".

### 4.4 Judging
1. Readings. Every judge reads every proposal at least four times; in reading k the proposals are ordered with the seed
   of judge_pass_k (config/study.yaml), and the calls of a stage are sent in one seeded random order that interleaves
   readings and proposals (section 9). One proposal per call: Dean et al. (2006, p. 668) presented ideas to each rater
   one at a time, so a score depends only on its proposal.
   The readings rule (`readings-check`). After a judge has read every proposal of the first wave four times, the
   single-reading reliability of each dimension the categories use (novelty, workability, relevance) is estimated on all
   proposals together, ICC(1,1), one-way random effects (Shrout and Fleiss, 1979), with nothing by structure entering.
   By Spearman-Brown, the smallest number of readings at which the reliability of the mean reaches .80 is
   K = .80 (1 - r) / (.20 r); the judge reads the largest K over the three dimensions, rounded up to an even number, no
   fewer than four and no more than eight. A dimension that would need more than eight is reported as below the
   reliability target, with every category that uses it; the judge then reads eight. The decision is made once per judge
   and experiment, before the internal pilot's recalculation (which needs the categories), and organizations added later
   are judged with the same number. The two judges are decided separately under the same rule. The floor of four: a
   judge at the edge of acceptable reliability (.50; Koo and Li, 2016) needs four readings to reach .80, and four
   readings put a dimension score on 25 grid values, so the threshold of 6 does not land on crowded values. The ceiling
   of eight is twice the floor (Mehta and Pocock, 2011).
2. The call. One call per proposal and reading rates all six subscales. The three dimensions appear in a random order,
   each followed by its two subscales in a random order, seeded by judge_order, the proposal's blind id, and the reading;
   the output schema lists the subscales in the same order, and the order is recorded in every call record and ratings
   row. A random order turns any order effect (Thorndike, 1920) into noise that the readings average. The judge reads,
   in this order: the rater instruction (Dean, Appendix C: apply the anchored levels exactly, one level per subscale),
   the same Article 12 text the writers and evaluators read, the stated problem with the decision frame, Dean's one-line
   definitions of the three dimensions (Table 8, p. 663), the anchored levels of the six subscales in the call's order,
   verbatim from Dean's Appendix A (Tables 21-25, pp. 686-690; the paradigm-relatedness levels are this study's Article
   12 version of Appendix B, as the instrument requires, p. 695), the protocol (one sentence of evidence first, then the
   integer level), and the proposal as JSON. The rubric states the reference class for rarity (the responses an
   experienced evaluator would expect AI R&D labs to give to this mandate). No examples per level are given.
3. The answer gives, per subscale, one sentence of evidence and the integer level. A call that fails after the three
   registered attempts leaves that reading of the proposal missing (8.4).
4. Dimension score = the mean over the judge's readings of (subscale A + subscale B): novelty = originality + paradigm
   relatedness, workability = acceptability + implementability, relevance = applicability + effectiveness, each 2 to 8
   (Dean's Table 11, p. 667). Specificity is not rated. The two judges are never averaged.

### 4.5 Order of operations
Each experiment is completed before the next one starts, the main experiment first: generation (84 per structure);
evaluation; the primary judge (four readings, the readings check, the remaining readings); the internal pilot's one
recalculation (6.4). If it extends, the added organizations are generated, evaluated, and judged, with the first wave's
ids and calls untouched. Then the second experiment, in the same order. After both experiments, with the internet on: one
small request to the second judge as a batch of one (not a study prompt; the model returned must be the configured one),
then for each experiment four readings of every proposal, its readings check, and the remaining readings. Then the
manifests and the analyses. No test is computed before every stage of an experiment is complete, the second judge
included (`analyze` refuses otherwise), and no organization or reading is added after any outcome by structure has been
seen.

## 5. Models and settings

### 5.1 Local models, offline; the second judge through the API
Every writer, evaluator, and primary-judge call is served by LM Studio on one computer (a Mac Studio, M5 Max, 64 GB)
from open-weight files whose SHA-256 hashes are recorded at preflight and in the manifest, with the network off. The
second judge rates the archived proposals afterwards through the Anthropic Message Batches API. The local instrument is
archived: a model identified by its file hashes can be rerun by anyone for as long as the files exist, at the cost of
machine time, with full control of sampling and of the chat template. The writers are not frontier models; whether the
effects transfer to frontier models is a question for a later study.

### 5.2 The line-up: a different developer for each job
The models were assigned to jobs from a separate benchmark of open models on tasks that share nothing with this study (no
registered prompt, proposal, structure, or rule): variation across copies and originality of ideas
for writers; accuracy, resistance to a wrong majority, and consistency when options are swapped for evaluators; and
test-retest reliability for judges. Qwen 3.8's copies disagreed far more often than Gemma 4's (13 against 2 of 16
equal-value items), and Gemma 4 followed a wrong majority far less often (1% against 22%) and kept its choice when options
were swapped. No model judges text from its own developer (LLM raters favor their own family's text: Panickssery,
Bowman, and Feng, 2024).

| job | main experiment | second experiment (replication) |
|---|---|---|
| writers | Qwen 3.8 27B (Alibaba), GGUF Q8_0, thinking at medium | Gemma 4 31B (Google), GGUF Q8_0, thinking on |
| evaluators | Gemma 4 31B, thinking on | Qwen 3.8 27B, thinking at medium |
| judges | Muse Glimmer 30B (Meta), primary, local; Claude Sonnet 5.5 (Anthropic), second, through the Batch API | the same two |

### 5.4 Judges
The primary judge is the more reliable of the local candidates by test-retest reliability (pass-to-pass Pearson r of
the dimension scores when the same proposals are rated twice) on 300 proposals written outside this study by another
model, provided no rating is missing after the registered attempts; nothing produced by this study's procedure entered
the choice. Muse Glimmer 30B (.74 / .53 / .73 for novelty, workability, and relevance, against .49 / .24 / .46 for the
next candidate) is the primary judge; its categories are used in every test. The primary judge is open weight, so that
the primary result can be replicated by anyone.

The second judge is Claude Sonnet 5.5 (Anthropic, `claude-sonnet-5-5`), a closed frontier model, through the Message
Batches API after the local run: thinking on, effort medium (the model's default is high), at most 10,000 tokens per
answer, no sampling parameters (the API accepts only their defaults), no system prompt, and no fallback to another
model. Every answer is archived with the data, so the analyses never need the model again. It rates the archived proposals of both
experiments with the same prompts, the same seeded orders, and the same readings rule, and every test is repeated with
its categories (7.4). Every pending call of a stage is submitted as one batch (at most 10,000 requests per batch); an
answer is matched to its call by the call's id; a request that errored, expired (unanswered after 24 hours), or was
canceled is a transport failure and is re-sent unchanged (8.1); an unusable answer is a content attempt, re-sent with the
identical prompt in the next batch, at most three in all; a batch left pending by an interruption is collected, never
submitted twice. Every answer is saved with the batch id, the times the batch was submitted and ended, and the full
answer (text, thinking summary, stop reason, tokens).

### 5.5 Settings common to all calls
- When no system message is sent, a judge's chat template inserts fixed lines of its own (Muse Glimmer: a generic
  assistant sentence, its knowledge cutoff, the current date, the reasoning level); the writers' templates add only
  formatting. These lines are the same for both structures, and the calls of both structures are interleaved, so none
  can differ by structure.
- Constraints the server's structured output does not enforce (integer ranges) are checked by the code (8.2); the number
  of items of each list is sent to the local server, whose grammar enforces it.
- LM Studio: one model loaded at a time; Qwen and Gemma with 4 parallel slots and a 49,152-token context, Muse Glimmer
  with 12 slots and a 98,304-token context, each context one pool shared by the slots (config/study.yaml, `load`). A
  request that does not fit while other slots are busy is refused by the server and re-sent like a server error (8.1).
  `preflight` checks each model while it is loaded: the identifier returned, the served folder, the hashes of every file
  in it (config/model_files.json), the context length and slots against the load recipe, and one tiny schema call.
- The network is off during the local stages; the software runs without it (uv in offline mode). LM Studio's automatic
  engine updates and just-in-time model loading are off; the runner stops if either is on, and the app and engine
  versions are recorded (A16).

### 5.6 Time
Estimates, from each model's speed on this computer applied to this study's prompt and answer lengths. Both experiments
at the first wave with four readings: generation about 1 to 1½ days, evaluation about 2 to 3½ days, judging about 2 to
2½ days, about 5 to 7½ days in all, unattended. An extension can add up to about one experiment's length; each reading
above four adds about a quarter of a day per experiment. The second judge takes a few hours to about 2 days.

## 6. Sample size

**A first wave of 84 organizations per structure in each experiment, then an internal pilot for H2: one blinded
recalculation after the primary judge has rated the first wave, extending the sample to the smallest size at which H2a and
H2b reach power .80, in whole allocation blocks, at most 168 per structure (6.4); one pre-drawn replacement per planned
organization (8.3).** The planning numbers come from power/run_sesoi.py (6.1, 6.2, 6.5) and power/run_h2_power.py (6.3).

### 6.1 The smallest effect of interest: d = .5
d is the difference between the structures in an organization's below-threshold share (H1), in units of the spread of
that share across organizations. d = .5 is also the equivalence bound (7.2); the closest registered precedent planned at
d = .35 to .5 (Piezunka and Schilke, 2023); .5 is Cohen's medium effect. No pilot number enters the plan (Albers and
Lakens, 2018): H1's power follows from d alone, and H2's recalculation uses the run's own data, pooled over the
structures (6.4).

### 6.2 H1
A two-sample t-test at d = .5, alpha .05 two-sided, needs 64 organizations per structure for power .80 (exact noncentral
t); at 84 the power is .90. The equivalence test at ±.5 concludes equivalence with probability .88 at 84 if there is no
effect.

### 6.3 H2
H2a and H2b pass through evaluation, so their power depends on quantities that only the run reveals: the category rates,
the dispersion of the counts written, the approval rates of each category under one of four and four of four, and the
dependence of approvals within an organization. H2 contains the difference between the two rules as well as the
generation difference, so it is powered whenever the rules disagree on a material share of proposals. The planning
simulation (power/h2_power.json) puts both above .95 at 84 per structure when the two rules approve, for example, 90%
and 20% of a category; when the four evaluators almost always agree (55% and 45%), H2b stays near .95 while H2a, which
the writing pulls against, can fall far below .80. Simulations draw each category in the direction H1 predicts for it.

### 6.4 The internal pilot for H2
Separately for each experiment, after the first 84 organizations per structure are generated, evaluated, and rated by the
primary judge (its readings decided, 4.4), frozen code (`extend-check`) makes one recalculation from quantities pooled
over both structures, never comparing them: the rate of quality and of below-threshold proposals, their approval rates
under each rule (among proposals with complete votes), the dispersion of the counts written (the lumped variance adjusted
for a structure difference of d = .5; Kieser and Friede, 2003), and the dependence of approvals within organizations. It
computes the exact power of H1 at d = .5 (.90 at 84; H1 never asks for more) and simulates the power of H2a and H2b
(4,000 simulated studies, each category drawn in H1's direction, the two structures centered on the pooled rate) at
alpha .05, at 84 and at 86, 88, ..., 168, and writes extension.json. The final size is the smallest size at which H2a and
H2b both reach .80, in whole allocation blocks of two per structure, at most 168 per structure. If the need exceeds 168,
the sample is 168, and a hypothesis below .80 is labeled underpowered, with its power, before any test; a
non-significant result on an underpowered test is inconclusive, never evidence of no effect. The added organizations are
pre-drawn in the same seeded allocation and are generated, evaluated, and rated before any test. The sample never
shrinks.
This is the standard internal-pilot design: one recalculation from blinded nuisance quantities (Wittes and Brittain,
1990, whose restricted design never goes below the planned size; Birkett and Day, 1994; Kieser and Friede, 2003; Friede
and Kieser, 2006), n = min(max(n1, nrec), nmax) with nmax prespecified. One recalculation avoids stopping at the first
estimate that happens to cross .80. Because the recalculation uses pooled quantities only, the type I error is
essentially unchanged. Only H2 is recalculated: H1's effect is stated in standard deviations, so the spread measured in
the first wave cannot change its required size.

### 6.5 What the sample cannot do
It cannot detect generation effects smaller than about d = .43 with power .80 at 84 and alpha .05 (about d = .31 at
168). A test labeled underpowered stays underpowered; the remedy is a new registered study, not more data added to this
one.

## 7. Analysis (src/np_registered/analysis.py)

### 7.1 Measurement
- Dimension scores from the primary judge (4.4): each dimension is the mean over the judge's readings of the sum of its
  two subscales, on Dean's 2-8 scale. A proposal with no rating on a subscale that a category needs is unclassified and
  left out of every test (8.4).
- Categories (Dean et al., 2006, pp. 675-677, equality passing): quality = workability ≥ 6 AND relevance ≥ 6; creative =
  quality AND novelty ≥ 6; below the quality threshold = NOT quality; novel = novelty ≥ 6 (secondary). Exclusive and
  exhaustive among classified proposals; proposals receiving the same ratings receive the same category. Reported with
  every analysis: the share of classified proposals exactly at the threshold and within half a point of it on each
  dimension, and the four cells of quality by novelty by structure.
- Votes: a proposal is approved under one of four if at least one evaluator approves and under four of four if all four
  approve; a proposal whose votes are incomplete is left out of the H2 counts and their denominators (8.4). Duplicate vote
  records of one proposal and role are counted once if they agree; if they disagree the analysis stops.
- Organization outcomes (12 proposals): below-threshold share, quality share, creative share, novel share, and the
  proposals of each category approved under each rule. An organization with no classified proposal contributes no
  observation to the tests of shares and is reported.

### 7.2 Confirmatory tests
- H1: Welch t-test of the proportion of proposals below the quality threshold generated, hierarchy minus polyarchy;
  estimate, 95% CI, Cohen's d. Only if the verdict is "not supported" (not after a reversal): a secondary equivalence
  test (two one-sided Welch tests at alpha .05) with bounds ±0.5 × the observed pooled SD.
- H2a (H2b): per organization, the number of its quality (below-threshold) proposals approved under its own rule (four
  of four for a hierarchy, one of four for a polyarchy), divided by its number of proposals with complete votes (twelve
  unless a vote is missing); Welch t-test, hierarchy minus polyarchy, predicted negative; 95% t interval and 95%
  percentile cluster-bootstrap interval (organizations resampled within structure, 10,000 resamples, seed 26100506).
  Reported with it: the decomposition of section 2 and the four cells (each structure's proposals under each rule).
- No correction for multiple tests: each hypothesis is tested and reported on its own at .05, two-sided, whatever its
  result. No conclusion rests on "at least one of" several tests; such a conclusion would need a correction, and
  individual claims do not (Rubin, 2021).
- The main experiment is the confirmatory test of H1, H2a, and H2b. The second experiment is a replication, analyzed the
  same way and reported separately as replicating, not replicating, or reversing; the experiments are never pooled.

### 7.3 Reported with every result (not tests)
- The rule comparison on the same votes (section 2): the mean over organizations with its 95% t interval and
  cluster-bootstrap interval, and the pooled approval rates of each category under each rule.
- Next to each registered test, the limitations that apply to it: underpowered at the final size (6.4), a high-attrition
  sample (8.3), and a category dimension below the reliability target (4.4).
- The internal pilot's recalculation; the dispersion of the counts written relative to binomial variation; the
  correlations of novelty with relevance and with workability; the dimension means by structure.
- Within each judge: the readings decision; ICC(1,1) and ICC(1,K) per dimension (Shrout and Fleiss, 1979); the Pearson r
  between readings 1 and 2; and the split-half agreement of the categories (readings 1-2 against 3-4, Cohen's kappa).
- Between the two judges, on each judge's mean of its readings: ICC(C,1) and ICC(A,1) (McGraw and Wong, 1996), the Pearson
  r and the disattenuated correlation, and Cohen's kappa of the categories.
- Evaluation: Fleiss' kappa among the four roles, Cohen's kappa for every pair of roles, and the share of proposals on
  which the roles disagree.
- Failures, per call log: attempts completed, content failures and their rate, first-attempt failures, outcomes obtained
  on a retry, exhausted calls, and the failure reasons, with the attrition statistics of 8.3 and the audit file of 8.4.

### 7.4 Secondary and exploratory analyses (labeled as such when reported)
Secondary:
- Sensitivity to the classification: every test with the second judge's categories, with the thresholds moved to 5 and
  to 7, and with the continuous scores in place of the categories (H1 on organization means of the shortfall
  8 - min(workability, relevance); the creative proportion on organization means of novelty; H2 on score-weighted
  approvals under the own rule: min(workability, relevance) for H2a, the shortfall for H2b).
- The creative proportion generated, hierarchy minus polyarchy (Welch).
- Novel proposals (Dean's third count): the proportion generated and the approvals under the own rule per proposal, by
  structure (Welch), and the four cells of quality by novelty.
- Creative yield: creative proposals approved under the own rule per proposal with complete votes (Welch).
- Manipulation check: per organization, the mean absolute distance between each junior's stance and the mean stance of
  the roles before it in the order of seniority; Welch t-test, hierarchy minus polyarchy, predicted negative.
- Mediation: the effect of structure on the H2a (H2b) outcome through the proportion of quality (below-threshold)
  proposals generated, in the framework of Imai, Keele, and Tingley (2010), with linear models at the organization level
  and the treatment-by-mediator interaction (the rule set by the structure changes the slope of the outcome on the
  mediator); ACME and ADE under each structure, their sum, and bootstrap intervals; the common-slope model with its
  sensitivity analysis (Imai, Keele, and Yamamoto, 2010). The structure also sets the rule, so the direct effect includes
  the effect of the rule.
- Reliability, agreement between evaluators, the share of proposals at the threshold, and proposal length by structure
  (words per proposal, the mean per organization with a Welch t-test, the correlation of length with each dimension, and
  H1 and the creative proportion with mean length as a covariate, HC2 standard errors).
Exploratory (no predictions): randomization inference for H1 and the creative proportion (permutation within allocation
blocks); proposal-level linear probability models; the first role in the order of seniority, which reads no one in
either structure, as a placebo; structure cues in proposal texts; the stance correlation by structure; error rates by
writing structure under each rule; role-level breakdowns; each judge's scores by their position in the call;
cross-evaluation by the other experiment's evaluator, if run; and illustrative examples chosen after the confirmatory
analysis.

## 8. Failures, exclusions, and missing data

### 8.1 Two kinds of failure
- Transport failure (rate limit, server error, timeout, connection error; for the second judge also a request that
  errored, expired, or was canceled in its batch): the identical request is re-sent with exponential backoff, at most 6
  attempts in all. If it still fails, the stage stops without recording any outcome for the unfinished calls and is
  resumed later with the identical requests. Resuming is automatic: the runner restarts the local server when a stage
  stops on a transport failure or writes no record for 45 minutes (each call has a 30-minute limit and is logged even when
  it fails, so only a hung server stays silent longer), reloads the stage's model with its load recipe, repeats
  preflight, and resumes, at most 5 times per stage. A transport failure never ends an organization and never produces a
  missing vote or rating.
- Content failure (the call completed but the answer is unusable: not valid JSON, a validity rule violated, a refusal,
  or output cut off at max_tokens): the identical prompt is re-issued with a new seed, at most 3 attempts in all (a
  convention). Every attempt is logged; a call that used up its attempts is recorded as exhausted and is not tried again
  when a stage resumes. With independent attempts at a per-attempt failure rate p, a call is exhausted with probability
  p^3: over the largest possible number of judge calls (two experiments, two judges, 168 per structure, eight readings:
  129,024 calls), fewer than one missing reading is expected for any p up to .020.
- Content failures never stop the run. Their counts are written into the run log after every stage and reported with the
  results by stage, model, and structure (7.3); a structure that fails more often shows in the attrition statistics and
  bounds of 8.3. The run stops only when continuing would be wrong: a registered stop (8.3), a loaded model that is not
  the registered one, changed LM Studio settings, a full disk, or a sixth failed recovery of one step.
- An interrupted write (a crash or power loss while appending) leaves an incomplete last line, which is skipped with a
  warning and its call run again; a resumed stage repeats no completed call; the logs are never edited.

### 8.2 Validity rules
A generation answer: exactly 3 proposals; every field a non-empty string; stance an integer 0 to 100; reasoning at least
15 words; each proposal (all four fields) at least 15 words; at least 4 distinct words in each text; no two proposals
identical. These rules catch degenerate outputs; they do not judge quality. The requested length of 60 to 110 words is not
enforced. Evaluation and judge answers must return the id sent, with a decision of approve or reject, a promise score
from 0 to 100, subscale levels in range, and non-empty texts.

### 8.3 Organizations
An organization is complete when all four roles produced a valid answer. A role that fails after the registered attempts
ends the organization, which is recorded in org_failures.jsonl and replaced by the next pre-drawn replacement of the same
structure; the replacement takes the block of the organization it replaces and records which one it replaced. The
analysis sample is, per structure, the completed organizations in allocation order, then completed replacements in
order, up to the target in force (84, or the internal pilot's size). There is one replacement per planned organization
(ids pre-drawn up to 168 per structure); a replacement is generated only when an organization fails. If more
organizations of one structure fail than the target in force, the run stops (a registered stop) and is reported; it is
then completed, as a deviation, with the organizations already complete.
Attrition is reported with every analysis: the failed organizations by structure with Fisher's exact test; the failure
rate by structure with the estimator for sampling until a fixed number of completions (Haldane, 1945); the overall and
differential attrition classified against the cautious boundary of the What Works Clearinghouse (Standards Handbook 4.1,
Table II.1), a sample outside it being labeled high-attrition next to every confirmatory test; and trimming bounds (Lee,
2009) on each structure contrast (H1, H2a, H2b), assuming monotone selection.

### 8.4 Proposals, votes, ratings
Evaluator and judge calls each hold one proposal, so a vote or a reading still missing after the three attempts is
missing. A missing vote is reported, and its proposal is left out of the approval counts (H2 and the rule comparison)
only; the H2 denominator is then the organization's number of proposals with complete votes. A missing reading is left
out of the proposal's average; a proposal with no rating on a subscale a category needs is unclassified. No outliers are
removed, no proposal is regenerated for quality, and nothing is excluded for content.
A content failure is a missing datum (Rubin, 1976); ignoring it is correct when the failure does not depend on the answer
the model was about to give. At analysis every content-failed attempt is written to results/failed_attempts_<log>.jsonl
with its reasoning text and the answer the same call later gave, and the share of outcomes obtained on a retry is
reported by stage and model.

## 9. Randomization and blinding
- Structure is assigned by seeded block randomization (4.1); organization ids carry no structure information and never
  appear in a prompt. Generation runs in allocation order, so the two structures are interleaved in time.
- Every proposal of a wave shares one seeded permutation of blind ids; an extension wave gets its own ids.
- Evaluators and judges see an opaque id and one proposal's text per call; no vote or rating depends on another proposal.
- The calls of every stage are sent in one seeded random order (seed schedule): evaluator roles and proposals, and judge
  readings and proposals, are interleaved, so a drift over the hours of a stage never lines up with one role, one
  reading, or one structure.
- `analyze` refuses to run before the internal pilot's decision exists and before every proposal has one vote from each
  role and every decided reading of both judges; `extend-check` uses the primary judge only and pooled quantities only.
- The analysis code is fixed and deposited before any data exist.

## 10. Data
- Every attempt of every call is logged in data/<arm>/calls_<stage>.jsonl with the full request (prompt, settings, seed),
  the full raw response (reasoning text included), the parsed answer, validity and the error of an unusable answer, the
  stop reason, the model id returned, token usage, latency, and the times the request was sent and answered (UTC; for
  the second judge, the batch id and the times the batch was submitted and ended). Home-directory paths and the
  computer's names are replaced in every record before it is written; nothing else is altered.
- Derived files: allocation.csv, orgs.jsonl, org_failures.jsonl, votes_<evaluator>.jsonl, ratings_<judge>.jsonl,
  readings_<judge>.json, extension.json, results/analysis_<judge>.json with a readable REPORT_<judge>.md, preflight.jsonl,
  and manifest.json (SHA-256 of the configuration, prompts, code, data, and model files, with package and engine versions).
- The record of the run: data/run.log (every line time-stamped: the runner's decisions, each step's output, every LM
  Studio command, the code version at the start), data/system.jsonl (every 5 minutes: memory, swap, load, disk, thermal
  state, the model loaded), and data/lmstudio_logs/ (LM Studio's server log for the session).
- The data are kept as written: after the run the data folder is committed to the study's repository (the raw call
  logs in compressed form) and deposited in the study's ResearchBox box; the complete call logs, failed attempts
  included, let the number of calls be checked against the design.

## 11. The pilot
A pilot study used earlier versions of Anthropic's Claude models; no pilot data are used in this study.

## 12. Assumptions
- A1. Role agents in one organization are separate, memoryless calls; organizations are independent draws given their
  structure and prompts.
- A2. The only manipulated difference between the structures is whether seniors' stances and reasoning are in the prompt
  (and the resulting difference in prompt length). "Hierarchy" at generation therefore means seniority-ordered influence
  through a summary judgment, the counterpart of Sah and Stiglitz's bureaus that "cannot communicate their actual
  information" (1986, p. 725); it is not authority, and the stance and reasoning are the treatment.
- A3. Evaluators' decisions depend on a proposal's structure only through its text; a text can carry a structure cue if
  a junior quotes a senior, which is measured (7.4).
- A4. The judge measures the same constructs in both structures (it is blind to structure and reads every proposal alone,
  under the same prompt, with orders drawn at random).
- A5. Dean's categories at the threshold of 6 define good (quality) and bad (below the quality threshold) projects; a
  category is a measurement, not a ground truth, which is why the second judge's categories and the thresholds 5 and 7
  repeat every test.
- A6. Planning values: d = .5 for the generation difference; for H2, the quantities observed in the first wave (6.4).
  Approvals that move together within an organization lower H2's power; the recalculation estimates this.
- A7. H2's power is computed by simulating the design, not from a formula.
- A8. The model files do not change during a run (hashed at preflight and in the manifest; the identifier returned is
  logged per call).
- A9. The experiments are reproducible at the level of files, prompts, settings, and logs, not of exact tokens; a
  different quantization or engine of the same weights is a close but not exact replication.
- A10. No vote or rating depends on another proposal (one proposal per call).
- A11. A missing vote is never read as a rejection or an approval.
- A12. The organization is the unit of analysis; proposals within an organization are never treated as independent
  observations in a confirmatory test.
- A13. The rule comparison compares the two rules on the same votes and needs no mediation assumption; its causal reading
  rests on the random assignment of structure and on evaluation blind to origin.
- A14. Sah and Stiglitz's assumptions that do not hold, stated in the paper: evaluators are heterogeneous (four
  functions); good and bad overlap; evaluation is simultaneous (committees, not sequential bureaus); evaluation cost and
  delay are not studied.
- A15. No correction over H1, H2a, and H2b; each is its own claim and is reported whatever its result.
- A16. Software: Python 3.12 with the versions pinned in uv.lock; LM Studio (0.4.26) with its llama.cpp engine (2.58.1);
  the preflight log and the manifest record the versions of the actual run.
- A17. The judges' templates add the current date to every call; the calls of both structures are interleaved, so the
  date cannot act differently by structure.
- A18. Readings of one judge are parallel measures (the model behind ICC(1,K) and Spearman-Brown), estimated on the
  study's own proposals.
- A19. A content failure does not depend on the answer the model was about to give (missing at random; Rubin, 1976),
  checked by the audit of 8.4 and bounded by 8.3.

## 13. Deviations
Recorded in DEVIATIONS.md: any change of a setting, a prompt, the code, or a rule after registration, and any unplanned
failure, with the date, the reason, and the data affected. Every deviation is also reported in the paper.

## 14. Software tests
The tests (tests/, `uv run --group dev python -m pytest -q`) run on a deterministic fake model and synthetic data:
prompt assembly and information flow (juniors see exactly their seniors' stances and reasoning and never their
proposals; polyarchy roles see nothing), allocation balance, the retry and replacement rules, resumption, blinding of
ids, one proposal per call, the judge's random orders followed by the output schema, the seeded schedule, the readings
rule, the internal pilot (pooled quantities only; the grid, the cap, and the underpowered label; extension waves that
leave the first wave untouched), the second judge's batches (expired requests re-sent, unusable answers re-sent as
attempts, no double submission), the order of the steps and the refusal to analyze early, the scrubbing of records, and
the analysis on synthetic data (false-positive rates at or below .05 under no effect; planted effects recovered with the
right sign; the categories exclusive and exhaustive; the identity H2 difference = generation part + rule part; the rule
comparison never positive).
